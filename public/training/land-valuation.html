"Value" is not one number

The same parcel has FIVE different values at the same moment — and they can be wildly far apart. Every pricing argument you'll ever have with a seller is really an argument about which value they're looking at.

Liquidationsold FAST — days, not months
Assessedthe county's TAX number
Marketfull exposure, no rush
Investmentone buyer's own math
Usevalue to one specific user
← usually lowestusually highest →
  • Liquidation value — what a property fetches when it must sell in a very short window (distress, foreclosure, "I need it gone"). Always below market. Speed costs money — that discount is the seller's trade, not a trick.
  • Assessed value — what the county taxes it on. It is not an opinion of market price; it's a formula, often a percentage of an old number.
  • Market value — the textbook one: the probable price, in cash, after reasonable time on the open market, with an informed buyer and seller and no pressure on either side. Notice how much of that definition is about TIME and NO DURESS.
  • Investment value — what one specific buyer can pay given the return THEY require. A builder who needs 20% profit has a different number than one who accepts 12%. Same dirt, different value.
  • Use value — value to a single user's special situation (the neighbor who needs your lot for their driveway). No market behind it — but occasionally your best exit.
⚠️
Blind-offer trap: assessed value can be DECADES old.

In California, Prop 13 (1978) froze tax values — they can only creep up ~2%/yr until the property sells. A parcel held since the 90s can be assessed at a small fraction of what it's worth. Other states reassess on their own cycles. Before you anchor a blind offer to ⅓ of assessed value, check when the parcel last changed hands. Long-held parcel → assessed is stale-low → your ⅓ offer may be insultingly low (or, sometimes, accidentally too high in states that assess near market). When in doubt, use ⅓ of estimated market value instead.

Our 3 offers = 3 different values

This is the whole game in one picture. We aren't giving a seller three random prices — we're letting them pick which value they want and what they'll trade for it.

CASH OFFER

Prices near liquidation value

Certain close, fast, as-is, no listing. The seller trades price for speed and certainty.

🗣️ "A cash price is a speed price. You're not selling the land cheap — you're buying back six months of your life."
DEVELOPER OFFER

Prices at investment value

Comes from the builder's own buy-box math — their finished-product revenue minus their costs, profit, and time.

🗣️ "This number isn't from comps — it's what a builder's spreadsheet says the dirt is worth to them."
NOVATION

Prices at market value

Full exposure on the open market — which is exactly why it takes 3–6 months minimum. Market value requires market time.

🗣️ "To get the top number, the market has to see it. Time on market isn't a bug of this option — it's the ingredient."
💡
Why this framing wins negotiations.

The appraisal definition of market value literally includes "reasonable exposure time" and "no duress." So when a seller wants a market price with cash-offer speed, they're asking for two things that cannot coexist by definition. Show them the ladder and let them choose the trade. No arguing, just physics.

The 6 ways pros price dirt

Appraisers have exactly six tools for land value. You already use #1 daily. The rest are your backup when there are no land sales — and #6 explains every developer offer you'll ever write.

1

Sales Comparison — the king

Recent sold land comps, adjusted, usually per acre or per buildable lot. When enough land sales exist, nothing else matters.

sold $/acre (grown to today) × subject acres = value
✅ OURS: this is the Cash (Land) calculator's comps mode.
2

Allocation — the lot % shortcut

In any neighborhood, the lot is a fairly stable slice of the finished home price (often ~20–30% for infill). No lot sales needed — just house sales.

new builds sell $400k × 25% lot share ≈ $100k lot
✅ OURS: fastest sanity check on an infill lot — grab the new-construction price next door and take the slice.
3

Extraction — subtract the building

Take a sold house, estimate what the structure is worth (replacement cost minus wear), subtract it — the leftovers are the dirt. Best on old/teardown houses where the building is worth little.

$310k sale − $60k tired house = $250k dirt
✅ OURS: perfect for teardown-heavy luxury infill streets — every old-house sale is secretly a land comp.
4

Ground Rent Cap — income ÷ rate

If the land itself earns rent (farm lease, billboard, cell tower, ground lease), divide that income by the market's land cap rate.

$6,000/yr farm rent ÷ 6% = $100,000
FYI: rare for us, but a leased-out parcel is worth MORE than the raw comps say — don't miss it.
5

Land Residual — what's left over

For income property: total income, minus the income the building must earn, leaves the income the land earns → divide by the land cap rate.

Income (I)Rate (R)Value (V)

Building must earn $900k × 7.5% = $67,500. The remaining $150k − $67.5k = $82,500 belongs to the land → ÷ 5.5% = $1.5M. (Memory hook: in the I-R-V row, ← multiply, → divide.)

FYI: know it exists — it's how commercial people talk. Not our daily tool.

How builders price bigger parcels: start from finished-lot revenue and subtract everything it takes to get there — including their profit and the cost of waiting.

Highest & Best Use — why "weed lot" ≠ weed-lot price

Land is valued at its best legal use, not its current use. Appraisers run every parcel through four tests, in order — each one filters the last.

1 · LEGALLY PERMISSIBLE — what does zoning actually allow? (the step-1 check on every deal)
2 · PHYSICALLY POSSIBLE — size, shape, slope, flood, utilities, access
3 · FINANCIALLY FEASIBLE — does building it actually pencil?
4 · MAXIMALLY PRODUCTIVE — of what's left, which use pays the most?

A "vacant weed lot" surrounded by new builds passes all four tests as a homesite → it's worth builder money, whatever it looks like today. A gorgeous parcel that fails test 1 or 2 (unbuildable zoning, no access, all wetland) is worth almost nothing. The dirt doesn't set the price — the best allowed use does.

Title insurance & Schedule B — where land deals die

Title insurance protects a buyer/lender against defects in ownership. The policy is not the interesting part — Schedule B is. It's the list of everything the policy REFUSES to cover, which on land is exactly the list of things that can kill a build.

The three habits:
  1. Open escrow → order the prelim EARLY. Schedule B surprises found in week 1 are negotiation ammo; found in week 5 they're a dead deal and wasted marketing.
  2. Read Schedule B line by line — and push back. Exceptions are not gospel: old easements can be removed, expired items deleted, liens cleared before close. You're allowed to question every line.
  3. NEVER throw the contract away — even years after closing. It's your proof of what was promised and insured if a claim ever surfaces.
🚩 Schedule B red flags on LAND (check every deal):
🚧
No recorded legal access

Landlocked parcels are near-worthless until an easement is bought. THE classic land trap.

⚡
Utility easement through the build area

A power/pipeline easement across the middle of a lot can shrink the buildable envelope to nothing.

⛏️
Mineral rights severed

Someone else may own what's under the surface — some buyers (and lenders) walk.

📏
Deed restrictions / HOA rules

Minimum square footage, no mobile homes, approval committees — they can void your buyer's plan.

💸
Unpaid taxes, liens, assessments

They close on top of your price. Know the payoff before you talk numbers.

👪
Breaks in the chain of title

Deceased owner, missing heirs, old unreleased deeds of trust — fixable, but it takes TIME. Price the delay in.

Turning this into seller conversations

Every common price objection is a value-ladder mix-up. Name the value they're quoting, then offer the trade.

Seller says
"Zillow / an online estimate says it's worth more."
"Those tools estimate market value — the price after months of full exposure with no time pressure. Our cash number is a speed price: close in weeks, as-is, zero fees, zero risk of it falling out. If time isn't a problem for you, our novation option chases that market number — it just needs 3–6 months to earn it."
Seller says
"The county says it's worth $X." (assessed value)
"That's the county's tax number, not a sale price — it comes from a formula, and depending on when you bought, it can run way below or above what buyers actually pay. Let's look at what land has actually sold for around you — that's the only number a buyer will honor."
Seller says
"A developer offered my neighbor way more."
"Developers pay investment value — their own spreadsheet: what finished lots sell for minus their build costs, their profit, and years of waiting. Two parcels a street apart can pencil completely differently. If your land fits a builder's box, we run that exact math — that's literally our developer offer."
Seller says
"I'm not in a hurry — I want full price."
"Then the honest answer is our novation: we list it to the open market, which is the only way to reach full market value — and we tell you upfront it takes 3–6 months minimum, because market value requires market time. Cash is for when weeks matter."
